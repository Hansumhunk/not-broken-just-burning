Six Sacred Questions · 05

Why?

Why is where explanation begins and mind-reading becomes tempting. Treat motive as a hypothesis until evidence earns something stronger.

Why? is often the question a hurt mind wants answered first: Why did they do that? Why did this happen? Why did I react that way? The problem is that motive is frequently the least observable part of the event.

A useful Why? investigation separates three layers: the reason someone gives, the reason you suspect, and the evidence available to support either explanation. Sometimes there are multiple motives. Sometimes the person themselves does not fully understand why they acted.

A plausible explanation is still an explanation until evidence makes it more.

What Why? is not: permission to diagnose, declare intent, or treat a pattern as proof of motive. “It fits” is a reason to investigate, not a reason to stop investigating.

Where it gets distorted: fear can fill missing information with the worst available explanation. Hope can do the opposite. Loyalty can excuse. Anger can convict. None of those emotions are useless, but none of them are independent evidence of another person's internal intent.

Use it under pressure: write at least two possible explanations for the same event, including one that does not require malicious intent. Then ask what evidence would distinguish between them. You are not required to choose the most flattering explanation. You are practicing epistemic discipline.

Guardian practice

Separate motive from impact.

Finish these sentences: What happened was... The impact on me was... My current explanation for why is... Evidence supporting that explanation is... Evidence I would need before calling the motive established is...
