Founder Lesson · 02

Boundaries are actions.

A boundary becomes useful when it tells me what I will do, not when it becomes an instruction sheet for another person's behavior.

Why this lesson matters

It is easy to call a demand a boundary: “You cannot do that.” Sometimes the concern is valid, but the sentence still places enforcement in somebody else's hands. A stronger boundary sounds like: “If this happens, here is what I will do next.”

That shift matters because it moves the center of gravity back toward agency. I may not control another person's choices. I can control access, participation, time, money, communication, location, or whether I remain in a situation.

A boundary is strongest when the action belongs to the person setting it.

What this is not

Boundaries are not punishment, revenge, silent tests, or threats designed to force compliance. They are also not walls around every uncomfortable feeling. A healthy boundary protects something specific while leaving room for honest connection where it is safe.

How boundaries get distorted

Fear can turn a boundary into total isolation. Anger can turn it into retaliation. Guilt can make us announce a boundary and then abandon it the moment somebody is disappointed. Clarity asks: what behavior crosses the line, why does it matter, and what action can I realistically maintain?

Practice

Write four lines: The behavior. The impact. My boundary. My action. Keep the final action entirely within your control. If it requires the other person to change before the sentence works, rewrite it.

Reflection questions

What am I actually protecting? Is my response proportional? Can I enforce this consistently? Am I creating safety, or trying to control uncertainty?
