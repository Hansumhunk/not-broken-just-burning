Stories From the Fire · Self-Definition

A State Is Not an Identity.

Overwhelmed, angry, shut down, afraid, dysregulated, exhausted: these can describe what is happening without becoming the entire definition of who you are.

Labels can be useful. A good label can give language to something confusing, help people communicate, point toward support, or explain a pattern that once felt random.

Labels become dangerous when description turns into destiny.

I am overwhelmed describes a state. I am an impossible person turns the state into identity. I reacted badly identifies behavior. I am broken makes the behavior a verdict.

Language should help you locate the problem, not erase the person around it.

This matters especially when emotions are intense. Strong emotion can narrow attention and change behavior. It may require accountability, regulation, rest, professional help, medication, boundaries, or repair depending on the situation. None of those possibilities require pretending the emotion is your entire character.

The same is true of clinical language. Diagnoses and professional descriptions can be valuable when responsibly used. They are not biographies. A diagnosis can describe a cluster of experiences without containing every value, relationship, strength, choice, skill, or possibility in the person receiving it.

Separating state from identity also improves accountability. If you believe a difficult reaction proves that you are permanently defective, shame takes over and learning gets harder. If you can say, I was flooded, I raised my voice, and I need a better plan for that moment, the behavior becomes specific enough to work on.

You are not required to deny difficult parts of yourself. You are asked to describe them accurately.

Accuracy creates room for change.

Reflection

Change “I am” into “I am experiencing.”

Take one harsh identity sentence and rewrite it as a temporary state, behavior, or need. Then ask what action belongs to that more accurate description.
