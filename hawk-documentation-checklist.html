Offline application

Turn an open question into a manageable task.

Download the self-contained HTML file and open it in your browser. No accounts, uploads, external scripts or automatic storage. Export before closing or reloading. JSON and printouts are unencrypted; store securely and avoid shared or monitored devices. Optional reference readers retain only IDs. The tool does not send requests, calculate legal deadlines, issue reminders or verify records.

For immediate danger contact emergency services; see Support & Safety. Consult qualified counsel about obligations and appropriate record requests.

Hard Evidence · 1 / 17

1. A checklist is a decision tool, not a demand for more records

The first two H.A.W.K. tools help you organize events and identify sources. The Documentation Checklist addresses the next problem: what remains to be done, and which of those tasks actually deserves your attention? A person going through separation can have a timeline full of unresolved questions and an evidence index full of references, yet still feel unable to decide where to begin. A list of every conceivable document may make that problem worse rather than better.

The original H.A.W.K. manuscript describes assembling a binder, organizing financial records and coordinating materials with counsel. This guide develops those practical ideas into a limited, prioritized work plan. It does not prescribe a universal collection list or assume that a person needs to assemble a case against another adult. A useful checklist might contain only three tasks: locate a notice, confirm an appointment and ask an attorney how to supply a record safely.

Begin by naming the practical objective. Do you need to prepare for a meeting, confirm a parenting responsibility or reconcile a particular expense? The objective determines what belongs on the list. If a task has no clear relationship to that objective, consider leaving it off. An unfinished list is not necessarily a failure. The important question is whether you know what deserves your next deliberate action.

2. Distinguish what you possess from what you need

A record that you already have requires a different action from a record you think might exist. If you possess a complete statement for the relevant period, your next action may simply be indexing it. If you have an excerpt, you may need to establish whether the omitted portions matter. If you have no record, you may need to ask a professional whether obtaining it is appropriate at all. These are different states, even when they all concern the same subject.

Start with your existing timeline and evidence index. Look at one unresolved question, then identify the source that might reasonably help answer it. Check your own authorized collection before creating a new request. You may discover that a document is already present under a different title or stored with another set of records. Assign or reuse its stable evidence identifier rather than collecting unnecessary duplicate copies.

Do not mark something as obtained merely because you remember seeing it. Equally, do not treat the absence of a record as evidence that someone withheld it. A missing item describes the current state of your working collection. It does not explain why that state exists. Record exactly what you know, including uncertainty. This habit makes the checklist a reliable planning instrument instead of a place where assumptions become accusations.

Hard Evidence · 3 / 17

3. Give each task a clear purpose and stopping point

A task should describe an action you can recognize as completed. Find out everything about the finances is not a manageable task. Locate my January statement and verify which period it covers is much more specific. Ask my attorney whether any additional statement is needed is a different task with a different owner. Each has an observable result and a reasonable point at which you can stop.

Use a short title that starts with the item or action. In the purpose field, explain the practical question it supports. This helps you decide whether a task is still useful if circumstances change. A document that seemed important during an earlier disagreement might become unnecessary when the issue is resolved. You should be able to close or defer that task without feeling that you have surrendered something.

Set a boundary around the scope. If the question concerns a particular appointment, avoid expanding the task into a review of every appointment for years. If a professional recommends a broader period for a legitimate reason, record that separate scope. A bounded task can be completed, reviewed or handed off. An unbounded task can consume any amount of time you give it. The objective is to regain command of the necessary work rather than let the work direct your entire life.

Hard Evidence · 4 / 17

4. Use statuses that describe reality

The checklist distinguishes a number of practical states: not started, in progress, requested, waiting for guidance, obtained or completed, deferred and not needed. These labels tell you what is happening with the work. They do not grade your character, assign blame to anyone else or establish a legal finding.

For example, requested should mean that you actually made an appropriate request through an authorized route. It should not mean that you are planning to make a request eventually. Waiting for guidance is useful when the next step requires legal, financial or safety advice. Obtained can mean that you received the specified material, but it does not mean the item has been authenticated or that its contents establish a particular proposition. Deferred means you have intentionally chosen to address the task later. Not needed means that the current purpose no longer warrants that action.

Write a short completion or deferral note so that a later review does not reopen the same question without a reason. You can also record a remaining question separately. A closed task can be reopened when something genuinely changes. It need not remain open forever merely because uncertainty exists somewhere in the wider situation. The status field is meant to reduce unnecessary cognitive load and help you recognize progress that would otherwise disappear in a growing collection of notes.

5. Prioritize by consequence, not by emotional intensity

During conflict, a painful message can feel more urgent than an ordinary appointment notice. Yet the practical consequences of missing an appointment or failing to obtain advice may be greater than the consequences of leaving that message unreviewed until next week. Priority is a choice about attention, not a measure of how much an experience hurt.

Consider three dimensions: whether the task affects immediate safety or a necessary obligation, whether it supports an upcoming decision, and whether it depends on something or someone outside your control. A task with a confirmed near-term obligation may need prompt attention. An item that is useful but not currently necessary may be routine. A hypothetical concern with no immediate action may be deferred. If you are uncertain about a legal obligation, ask qualified counsel promptly rather than assigning a deadline yourself.

The tool provides a simple priority field so you can sort tasks. It does not determine urgency automatically or tell you what your legal responsibilities are. A date approaching in a worksheet is not an official deadline alert. Verify important dates in the actual authoritative source and use an appropriate separate reminder or calendar. The checklist is designed to support judgment, not substitute for it. You decide what matters in context, and qualified professionals can advise where specialized obligations are involved.

6. Separate confirmed obligations from personal target dates

A date can mean many things in a work plan. It might be the date you hope to finish sorting a folder, a date you plan to follow up with your own advisor, or a date supplied in an official communication. These are not interchangeable. A personally selected target can often be adjusted; an actual obligation may have consequences that require professional advice.

When you write a target date, identify its basis. Is it your own planning decision, a date a professional asked you to use, or a date copied from an authoritative notice? If it is the latter, preserve the notice and ask counsel about any uncertainty. Do not calculate procedural deadlines from a general website guide or assume that a date mentioned in an email has a particular legal effect. The checklist cannot interpret orders, determine how time is counted or automatically account for changes in a case.

A task without a date can still be legitimate. You may be waiting for advice before deciding when to act. Leave the field blank rather than inventing precision. If a date changes, update the working task and record the reason. Keep a separate reliable calendar for verified obligations. The practical value of the date field is to make your own review more organized, not to produce a false sense that every important deadline is covered.

Hard Evidence · 7 / 17

7. Identify an appropriate route before requesting anything

The fact that a record would be useful does not create a right to obtain it. Begin by identifying where the record is legitimately available to you. It may be your own authorized account, a document already supplied to you, or material that your attorney or another qualified professional can help you request. You may also need to conclude that obtaining a particular item is inappropriate or unnecessary.

Record the route in ordinary language. For example: download my own statement, ask counsel about the correct request process, or check the copy already provided. Avoid logging passwords, security answers, private account details or another person's location. Do not take an entry on a checklist as permission to access another adult's device, use tracking tools, contact a restricted party or collect material through a child or intermediary.

The application does not send requests or contact anyone. It provides a space to describe an action you have chosen or already taken lawfully. If you are not sure whether a request is permitted, change the status to waiting for guidance and write down the specific question. It is better to leave a record missing than obtain it in a way that creates risk or violates someone else's rights. Disciplined preparation includes knowing when an action should not be taken without advice.

Hard Evidence · 8 / 17

8. Create a communication record without manufacturing conflict

If a legitimate request is made, note the date, the route and the actual outcome. A simple record might say that you asked your own attorney whether there was a complete copy of a notice and were told that counsel would review the file. That is enough to establish your next action. You do not need to assign a motive to a delayed response or interpret a request status as evidence of wrongdoing.

Keep the distinction between an intended action and a completed action visible. A planned request is not a sent request. A sent request is not a receipt. A receipt is not confirmation that every question has been resolved. If the response includes a document, you can index it separately and attach its reference code to the checklist task. If a response clarifies that the document is not needed, consider closing the task.

Do not use the checklist to schedule repeated contact with a person who has asked you to stop, circumvent a communication restriction or escalate an argument. If an important issue remains unresolved, use the appropriate professional or permitted channel. A follow-up is useful when it serves a defined purpose and respects the other constraints of the situation. Sometimes the correct next action is to wait for a professional response, attend to another responsibility and avoid sending a new message simply because waiting is uncomfortable.

Hard Evidence · 9 / 17

9. Connect the checklist to events and evidence

The tools serve distinct functions. A timeline records a relevant event and the basis of your knowledge. An evidence index identifies a source and its limitations. The checklist tracks what you intend to do about an appropriate gap, obligation or question. Keeping those jobs separate prevents a task from being confused with an event or a missing document with a verified record.

Suppose the timeline contains a change to a scheduled appointment. The evidence index identifies the original notice but you have not established whether an updated notice was supplied. A checklist item could say: determine whether a revised notice exists through my authorized records or counsel. Link the event identifier and the existing evidence identifier to show why the task exists. When an updated notice is obtained legitimately, create an index entry and add that new reference as appropriate.

The documentation checklist can read the identifiers from saved timeline and evidence JSON files. It retains only the identifiers in memory for reference checks. It does not modify the other tools or automatically synchronize their records. A reference appearing in the selected export shows that the identifier is present in that file, not that the associated claim is true. A missing reference could simply mean you opened an older export. Keep versioned files and choose the correct ones for your current purpose.

10. Organize parenting tasks around a child's practical needs

When children are involved, start with what helps their daily life function. School information, agreed schedules, medical instructions and transportation arrangements may be relevant to practical coordination. Which specific records are appropriate to obtain will depend on your responsibilities, permissions and circumstances. A checklist should not encourage you to build a portfolio of every perceived mistake made by another parent.

A useful task might be to confirm the date and place of an appointment through a permitted source, or to locate a copy of the school schedule you are entitled to receive. The outcome is a concrete improvement in your ability to show up for your child. If a record concerns a child's private health, educational or personal information, minimize what you enter into a general working list and use secure storage for the original material.

Do not ask children to retrieve records, photograph another household or repeat conversations for the purpose of building evidence. If a child tells you something concerning, attend to their welfare and seek appropriate guidance. An immediate safety concern requires real-world action, not completion of a worksheet. The checklist can help organize ordinary responsibilities, but it is not a safety monitor or a substitute for qualified assistance. A father's success should not be measured by the amount of conflict-related documentation he has collected. It includes the ordinary, dependable care he provides.

11. Scenario one: preparing for a professional meeting

Imagine that Jamie has a meeting with an attorney on Friday. Jamie possesses two notices, has a timeline of three events and is uncertain whether another notice exists. The initial impulse is to spend the entire week trying to gather every document associated with the separation. Instead, Jamie writes a small plan based on the purpose of the meeting.

The first task is to locate the two existing notices and ensure that each has a usable reference in the evidence index. The second is to prepare a short list of questions about the relevant events. The third is to ask counsel whether an additional notice is necessary and, if so, what the proper method of obtaining it would be. Jamie assigns the first two tasks personal target dates and sets the third to waiting for guidance. The actual meeting date remains in a separate calendar.

When counsel responds that one of the notices is unrelated to the issue under discussion, Jamie marks the associated task not needed and explains the reason. The original document is not destroyed. The working plan becomes smaller and more useful. This is the desired outcome: arrive at the meeting with relevant material and specific questions, rather than an exhausting stack of unrelated records. The checklist helped Jamie allocate attention without offering legal advice or determining what a court would accept.

Hard Evidence · 12 / 17

12. Scenario two: clarifying a household expense

Consider Quinn, who has a statement showing a charge that Quinn does not recognize. The timeline includes the transaction date and the evidence index references the statement. Quinn wants to know whether a receipt or other record clarifies the charge. The missing information is specific, so the checklist begins with a bounded task rather than a broad investigation into another person's activities.

Quinn first reviews records already available through authorized access. If a relevant receipt is found, Quinn indexes it and records a next action: compare the two records or ask an appropriate professional to help with the reconciliation. If the receipt cannot be found, Quinn records that limitation and asks counsel or a qualified accounting professional whether any further request is appropriate. The task's status becomes waiting for guidance rather than evidence withheld.

Suppose the later review establishes that the charge was an ordinary household expense. Quinn marks the task completed, links the supporting record and writes a short outcome note. There is no reason to keep the task active simply because it initially felt suspicious. If the question remains unresolved, that too can be recorded accurately. The checklist has not decided whether any wrongdoing occurred. It has transformed a vague concern into a legitimate action, a defined outcome and a reasonable stopping point.

Hard Evidence · 13 / 17

13. Scenario three: an appointment that needs confirmation

Imagine Casey has heard that a child's appointment may have changed. Casey needs to plan transportation but has not seen an updated schedule. The immediate practical question is when to arrive, not why the schedule may have changed. Casey enters a task titled confirm appointment details through the permitted channel. The priority reflects the approaching appointment and the need to arrange transportation.

Casey looks at the information already provided and uses the permitted communication route to request clarification. The checklist records that a request was made, without reproducing unnecessary private medical information. Casey keeps the actual appointment time in a separate calendar and takes any required steps to make sure the child's needs are met. If a revised notice is received, a minimal evidence reference can connect the relevant document to the task.

Once the appointment is confirmed, Casey marks the task completed and records the practical outcome. There is no need to add a new request about every previous appointment. If communication restrictions apply, Casey follows those restrictions and asks an appropriate professional for help rather than finding a workaround. If the child's immediate health or safety is at risk, Casey uses urgent real-world assistance. In this scenario, the tool succeeds because the child receives dependable care and the parent has a manageable plan, not because the parent has gathered more information about the other household.

14. Review the list at a deliberate interval

A checklist is most useful when it can be reviewed without taking over the entire day. Set a short review session before a relevant meeting or at another reasonable interval for your situation. Start with the tasks that have practical consequences, verified obligations or dependencies. Look for a next action you can actually complete. Then close the application when you have made that choice.

During a review, ask whether each open task still serves its stated purpose. Has the question been answered elsewhere? Has a professional advised that no further record is needed? Is the task waiting on a response that you cannot control? Is there a simpler step that would fulfill the objective? Changing a status to deferred or not needed may be a wise use of judgment rather than a retreat.

Do not interpret a completion percentage as a measure of your worth or the strength of your situation. A checklist with two necessary tasks completed can be more useful than a long list with eighty unnecessary tasks checked off. This tool reports working task counts, not the likelihood of a legal outcome. If you find yourself adding more tasks whenever you feel distressed, step away and consider using a Flamewalker reflection tool or speaking with someone qualified to support you. You are building an organized life, not trying to achieve a perfect archive.

15. Protect working plans and preserve relevant records

A planning list can contain sensitive information even when you do not attach documents. A storage alias, a question for counsel or a reference to a child's appointment may reveal private details in context. Use minimal descriptions, avoid unnecessary identifiers and consider who can access your device, printed copies or exported files. Do not assume that possession of a password or a private label is enough to protect everything.

This offline application keeps task entries in memory during your session. You must explicitly export JSON if you want to reopen the plan later. That file is ordinary readable data, not encrypted by the tool. Use storage appropriate to your circumstances, and be mindful of shared computers, synced downloads folders and printers. A person who accesses an exported file might be able to read the entries. Seek individualized safety guidance if working with records could put you at risk.

Editing a task changes your working plan, not the underlying document or event. Preserve original records, prior versions when necessary and anything subject to applicable preservation instructions. Do not delete materials because you changed a task's status to not needed. The program can clear its current session, but it cannot remove files already saved elsewhere. If you need advice about what must be retained, how to share it or what may be confidential, consult a qualified professional before taking action.

16. Guided exercise: build a small completed work cycle

Use fictional data before entering sensitive information. Create a timeline with one event and an evidence index with one related record. Export both files. Open those exports in the checklist and confirm that the displayed identifier counts match the records you created. You are practicing reference checks, not authenticating documents or merging the tools into a single database.

Create three tasks: organize the record you already possess, ask a fictional advisor whether another record is needed, and complete an ordinary planning action. Give each a short purpose, a priority, an appropriate route and a realistic next step. Set an optional personal target date for one task. Link the existing event and evidence IDs where they are relevant. Try a malformed identifier and observe that the application rejects it. Then try a well-formed identifier that does not appear in the selected export and observe the difference.

Change the first task to completed with an outcome note. Set the second to waiting for guidance, then simulate a response and mark it not needed with a reason. Leave the third open or defer it with an explanation. Filter your active tasks, export the entire plan and reopen it in a fresh session. Verify that statuses and references persist. The exercise is successful when you can tell what is finished, what is open and why. End the practice by putting the checklist aside and doing something that supports your life outside the conflict.

Hard Evidence · 17 / 17

17. Turn preparation into agency

The H.A.W.K. Hard Evidence phase begins with understanding events, then identifying relevant sources, then deciding what legitimate work remains. Those three activities are useful precisely because they can be finished in small portions. They do not require you to make every uncertain detail certain, acquire every imaginable document or resolve the entire past before moving forward.

At the end of a review, select one action that respects your obligations, permissions, priorities and the wellbeing of those affected. If you need advice, asking the right person a precise question can be the most productive action. If you already have the necessary material, stop collecting and use it appropriately. If a task no longer matters, allow yourself to close it. Nothing in the H.A.W.K. system requires you to keep yourself in a state of constant vigilance.

The Flamewalker Path addresses another part of this process: how you rebuild your identity and direct your energy when the external situation is difficult or unresolved. The checklist cannot accomplish that personal work for you. What it can do is reduce avoidable confusion so you are free to attend to your responsibilities, relationships, health and future. Your progress is not the number of files you collect. It is your growing ability to choose a useful next step and return to a life that is larger than the dispute.
