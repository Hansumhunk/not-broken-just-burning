You may be managing a separation, a loss, work pressure, isolation, or a difficult change in your identity. You do not need to diagnose yourself or have the perfect words before asking for help. Start with what is affecting your life today.

Safety first

Need help right now?

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call local emergency services (911 in the United States). If you are experiencing suicidal thoughts or emotional crisis in the U.S., call or text 988 or visit the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. If abuse or coercion is affecting your safety, a trained advocate can help you plan. Outside the U.S., use local emergency and crisis resources.

Stress and overwhelm

When every task feels urgent, identify the one that actually needs attention. A short list of current obligations, body needs and available support can help you decide what is manageable today.

Anger and emotional pressure

Notice the moment between a feeling and an action. Delay nonurgent conflict, move to a safe setting and choose a response aligned with your responsibilities. If you fear losing control or harming someone, seek immediate help.

A practical starting point

Make the next day more manageable.

Instead of treating health as another performance target, choose one basic need and one realistic action. Make time for food, water, rest, movement you can safely do, and contact with someone you trust. You do not have to do all of them today.

Write down what is interfering with daily life, what you can influence, and one person or service you could contact. Keep private notes somewhere secure. If you want a guided reflection, explore Flame Check-In or the free tools.

Self-care can support wellbeing but is not a substitute for treatment. If symptoms persist, worsen, or interfere with relationships, work or everyday functioning, contact a qualified healthcare professional.

Professional support

When should you talk to a professional?

Asking for help is a practical decision, not a test of manhood. If distress makes everyday tasks difficult, a healthcare provider can help assess what is happening and discuss appropriate care. You can bring a short list of concerns and questions to an appointment.

For general mental health information and help-seeking guidance, read the National Institute of Mental Health: Caring for Your Mental Health and My Mental Health: Do I Need Help?. These sources provide health information; NBJB does not provide clinical services.

For support with relationship safety, the National Domestic Violence Hotline offers information and advocate contact options.
