1. An index is a map, not a verdict

Imagine that you have a folder containing three hundred screenshots, a pile of printed documents, several bank statements and a calendar full of notes. You know that some of those items matter, but when someone asks you a specific question, you cannot find the one document you remember seeing. The problem is not necessarily a shortage of information. It is that your collection has no map. An index provides that map by assigning each relevant item a reference, recording what it is and pointing you toward the original.

The original H.A.W.K. manuscript emphasizes dated records, exhibits, metadata and organized documentation. The purpose of this guide is to turn those ideas into a careful, usable system. An index is not a declaration that every item establishes the truth of a claim. It is also not a substitute for the actual records. It helps you locate a source, understand how you obtained it and communicate a question about it.

Begin by asking what you need to accomplish. You may need to prepare for an appointment with your attorney, reconcile a household expense or understand a change in a parenting arrangement. Your collection should serve those needs, rather than expanding simply because you are worried that something could be relevant later. An organized collection of ten useful records can be far more valuable than a thousand unidentified screenshots.

2. Define what belongs in the collection

Establish the subject and period before collecting more material. If you are trying to understand a disagreement about a payment made last month, start with the records that describe that payment. Do not assume that every unrelated communication from the last several years belongs in the same collection. A private journal can remain separate from an index intended for a professional meeting.

Determine whether an item is relevant, whether you are permitted to access it and whether you already have it. Permission is not created by being married, separated or involved in litigation. Work with your own records, communications you legitimately received or material provided through appropriate channels. If you need records you cannot lawfully access, bring that question to your attorney rather than attempting to obtain them yourself.

It is equally important to avoid collecting material in a way that creates more conflict. You do not need to provoke an exchange to have something to record. Do not follow another person, secretly access an account, enlist children to obtain information or pressure someone into producing a statement. In many ordinary cases, the records already available to you are sufficient to identify the next useful action. A missing item can remain missing while you ask what legitimate options exist.

3. Separate the original, the working copy and the index

An original in this guide means the source material you are preserving, such as a message export as received, a downloaded statement or an original paper notice. The word does not mean that the tool has authenticated that material. An original may itself contain an error, incomplete information or a disputed assertion. Preserve it without treating its contents as automatically accurate.

A working copy is a separate version created for convenience. You might make a duplicate for annotation, an excerpt for discussion or a redacted version for a particular audience. Label that copy as a copy and keep its relationship to the source visible. Avoid changing the only available version. If the context of a message matters, a short excerpt should not be mistaken for the whole conversation.

The index is a third thing: a small description and a locator. It may contain a reference code, title, type, date, origin, storage alias, context and relevant event references. It should not contain an entire document or sensitive attachment. If an item is unavailable, make that status clear. The index does not replace the item merely because you have written a detailed description of it. Think of the index as a library catalog: a catalog entry tells you where to find a book, but is not the book itself.

Hard Evidence · 4 / 17

4. Give each item a stable identifier

A useful identifier should remain the same even if you correct the title, update the location or change the category. In an informal paper notebook, you may have used sequential codes such as E001. The digital Evidence Index generates a longer stable code for each new item. That code can be copied into the timeline without depending on a row number or the order in which you display the records.

Avoid reusing the same code for different items. If you remove a working entry, its identifier should not be reassigned to a new document simply because there is a gap in your numbering. If you create an entry for a later version or a separate source, describe its relationship to the first item in the notes. A stable identifier lets you discuss a document with less confusion even when the collection changes over time.

You may already have an older reference system from a binder or attorney. Keep those original codes as legacy aliases in your notes rather than renaming official exhibits or overwriting someone else's filing system. The internal HAWK identifier does not assign an exhibit number in a court proceeding. An attorney can advise when and how a document is formally designated. The practical goal is that you can find the same record tomorrow that you were discussing today.

Hard Evidence · 5 / 17

5. Dates describe different things

A record may have more than one important date. A message was sent on Monday, exported on Wednesday and received from a professional on Friday. A bank statement covers a month but was issued after that month ended. A letter might be dated earlier than the date it arrived. Do not collapse those different dates into a single claim about when the underlying event happened.

In the index, record the date shown on the item when known and distinguish it from the date you obtained the item. If the source is a month-long statement, the month may be more meaningful than a guessed day. Use the context field to explain what the date represents. If you cannot establish it, mark it unknown instead of entering a convenient placeholder.

A date displayed by an application may depend on local time settings. Preserve any relevant time or time zone exactly as it appears in the source, rather than replacing it with a guessed conversion. Do not confuse the file creation date with the time of an event inside the document. If the distinction matters, preserve both and seek appropriate technical or professional guidance. An accurate index should make timing questions easier to ask, not create confidence where the underlying material is ambiguous.

6. Record where the information came from

Describe the origin of an item in ordinary language. Was it a statement supplied by your financial institution, a message you personally received, a document mailed to you or a copy provided by counsel? A useful origin note helps you distinguish the actual source from the person who later forwarded a copy. It also makes the limits of your knowledge visible.

Be particularly careful with secondhand material. If someone gives you a printed screenshot of a conversation, you can describe the screenshot and how you obtained it without declaring that you independently reviewed the original conversation. A forwarded document may be genuine, incomplete or mislabeled. Do not silently promote a copy to an original merely because it contains information you find persuasive.

If an item came from an official record, record the relevant issuing organization or a neutral alias, as appropriate. You do not need to include confidential personal identifiers to explain the origin. If a professional has supplied documents under restrictions, follow those restrictions and ask how you should store or share them. The index's source field is for provenance in the ordinary sense of where a thing came from. It is not a certification of authenticity, a formal chain of custody or a determination of admissibility.

Hard Evidence · 7 / 17

7. Preserve context and be careful with metadata

The manuscript emphasizes timestamps and metadata. Those details can help clarify where a file came from or when it was created, but metadata is not a guarantee that a document is accurate. File information may change when a file is copied, exported or converted. An application may display a timestamp based on its own settings. A printed copy may omit fields that are available in the original electronic version.

Whenever possible, keep the underlying material intact. If you legitimately received a message, preserve the surrounding exchange when it is relevant instead of selecting only the most upsetting sentence. If you work from a printout or excerpt, describe what you have, not what you assume the missing material contains. If you cannot locate the complete item, identify that limitation rather than recreating a version from memory.

This tool does not inspect or extract metadata. It does not perform forensic acquisition, calculate file hashes or validate a document's origin. If authenticity, technical preservation or forensic examination is important, ask a qualified professional how to proceed before altering or transmitting the file. A well-maintained index can help that professional understand what you have and where it came from. It cannot do the professional's job. Resist the temptation to claim technical certainty based solely on a filename, date displayed by a device or confident description.

Hard Evidence · 8 / 17

8. Write useful descriptions without deciding the outcome

Give each record a short recognizable title. A title such as April 10 exchange message is more useful than proof of manipulation number seventeen. The first title identifies a source. The second attempts to assign a conclusion before the reader has examined it. A neutral category, such as communication or finances, makes it easier to find related items without incorrectly treating them as findings.

In the description, state what appears in the record. If an email requests a change of appointment, say that. If a statement shows a charge, identify the transaction and whether its purpose is known. If a notice describes a requirement, preserve its wording or refer to the exact document for a professional to interpret. Do not use the index to declare that a person committed a crime or that a legal provision necessarily applies.

Use the limitations field to record what the material cannot establish. A receipt does not necessarily show who ultimately used an item. A calendar invitation does not establish that an appointment actually occurred. A message requesting a change does not always explain the reason for that request. These limitations are not reasons to discard the records. They help you decide how to ask the next sensible question and avoid attributing more meaning to a document than it can support.

Hard Evidence · 9 / 17

9. Organize storage without exposing the documents

Consider using a simple storage structure under your own control. Separate source materials from working copies and keep your index in an appropriate secure location. A storage alias like folder B, item 12 may be sufficient to locate a document without putting a full local path, account number or private address in the index. Choose a method that you will still understand several weeks later.

The offline application never asks you to upload a source attachment. It stores text entries in memory while the tool is open and lets you deliberately export a JSON file. This exported file is not encrypted. A person who can access it may be able to read your notes. Do not assume that a private label provides protection by itself. Use appropriate secure storage and avoid shared or monitored devices if using them would put you at risk.

Also consider what happens when you move or rename a document. Update the locator in the working index and preserve any required previous versions. Check that the new location points to the intended material instead of a similarly named file. Do not use a browser's downloads folder as your only archive if files there are automatically synchronized, shared or regularly cleared. In a dangerous relationship or living arrangement, obtain individualized safety advice before undertaking any record storage or transfer.

10. Connect one item to one or more timeline events

The timeline and index answer different questions. The Incident Timeline Builder helps you organize what happened in chronological order. The Evidence Index helps you identify the material that may inform those entries. An event can have more than one source. A single document may also relate to several events. Connecting those entries makes both collections easier to navigate.

Start by creating the timeline event using the most accurate date and description you can support. Add the evidence item to the index, then copy the generated evidence reference into the timeline's reference field. The evidence item can carry the stable timeline event references in its own linked events field. You can open a saved timeline export within the Evidence Index to check whether the referenced event identifiers exist. Only the event identifiers are retained by this import operation; it does not combine or alter the timeline itself.

A link means related, not proven. If two items disagree about an event, you can link both and explain the difference. Do not remove a relevant item just because it complicates your account. A missing link may simply be work you have not completed. An unrecognized event identifier may mean you have opened an older or different timeline export. Do not assume the event never happened because a particular local file does not contain it.

11. Scenario one: a message and a calendar entry

Imagine Cameron is trying to understand a disputed pickup time. Cameron has an original message conversation that requests moving the exchange from five to six thirty and a calendar entry showing the original planned time. Both records are relevant but describe different things. The calendar records a planned arrangement, while the conversation records a request to change it.

Create two evidence entries, each with its own reference. The first might be titled April exchange message conversation, categorized as communication and described as a complete export of the relevant conversation. The second might be titled Planned pickup calendar entry. Indicate whether the calendar source is an original account export, a screenshot or a copy. Both items can be connected to the same timeline event.

Suppose Cameron later discovers another message accepting the requested change. That information belongs in the record as well. If the complete conversation already includes it, update the description with a correction note rather than creating unnecessary duplicate entries. If the acceptance is contained in a separate legitimate source, index it independently and link it to the relevant event. A useful account includes the request, the reply and the resolution when those are available. The index does not need a claim about why either adult acted. It gives Cameron a manageable way to prepare for a practical discussion.

12. Scenario two: two records that do not agree

Consider Devin, who has a statement showing a charge and a receipt showing a similar amount on a nearby date. Devin believes the records may refer to the same purchase but cannot confirm the connection. The safest organizational approach is to create two separate records. Give each an accurate description, identify its source and describe the relevant dates separately.

In the limitations field, write that the match is not established. In the related events field, link the transaction event if it is relevant to the question. Avoid entering an unsupported amount as certainly missing or claiming a fraud finding. There may be a difference in processing dates, a refund, a second purchase or a genuinely unresolved discrepancy. A qualified accounting professional or attorney can help determine what is appropriate to investigate or request.

If a later payment confirmation establishes a relationship between the two documents, preserve the new source and note what changed in your understanding. If it does not, keep the question open. It is perfectly acceptable for an index to contain a set of records that has not yet been reconciled. The useful output is a concrete question with identifiable references, not a dramatic conclusion. This method can save time by helping a professional see the actual source materials rather than reconstructing a concern from scattered descriptions.

13. Scenario three: a secondhand screenshot

Imagine Riley receives a screenshot from a friend who says it shows a conversation relevant to Riley's situation. Riley did not participate in that conversation and has not seen the original application or the rest of the exchange. The friend may be trying to help, but Riley does not know whether the screenshot is complete, accurate or appropriately shared.

Riley can describe the item as a screenshot supplied by another person, if it is lawful and appropriate to retain. The origin field should reflect how the item was received. The status should indicate a copy rather than an original. The limitations should state that the full exchange has not been reviewed and the underlying source has not been independently verified. If it is not appropriate to retain the material, do not add it merely to fill out the collection.

The friend should not be asked to access another person's account, solicit private information or change their statement to fit Riley's interpretation. If the material might have genuine legal significance, Riley can ask counsel how to handle it, including whether it should be retained or shared. This is a good example of why source classification matters. Your personal knowledge is not the same as the information another person says they have. An index should preserve that distinction without dismissing or exaggerating the possible relevance of the item.

14. Handle missing records, duplicates and corrections

An index may contain a record that you know exists but do not currently possess. Mark it as referenced only or not located, and explain why it appears in the collection. A request from your attorney for a specific financial statement is not the statement itself. Do not create substitute documents from memory or mark missing information as verified. The Documentation Checklist provides a separate place to track appropriate next steps for missing items.

You may also discover duplicate copies of a record. If they are the same item, a single index entry may be sufficient. If the versions differ, they may deserve separate identifiers with an explanation of their relationship. Check before deleting working notes. If preservation obligations or professional instructions apply, follow those rather than assuming you may discard duplicate materials.

When correcting an entry, explain what changed and why. Perhaps the issue date was wrong, the storage alias moved or you learned that an item was a copy rather than an original. The application preserves the stable reference and records the latest edit time. It is an editable catalog, not an immutable log. Keep separate versioned exports when appropriate. Do not use the tool to conceal earlier material or destroy original documents. A changed understanding is normal; a transparent correction is a sign of disciplined work.

15. Prepare a useful professional handoff

Before sending a collection to a professional, ask what they actually need and how they prefer to receive it. A brief index of relevant items may be sufficient for an initial discussion. Your attorney may want the full original documents through a secure channel rather than an unprotected spreadsheet or email attachment. A professional can help decide what should be included in a formal filing or disclosed to someone else.

Use the filter to prepare a short displayed list of records for a focused discussion. Printing the displayed entries is different from exporting the full index. A printed document may include sensitive notes, so review what is visible before producing a copy. If you need to share only a selection, discuss the appropriate method and redactions first; a full JSON export contains all current index entries regardless of the filter.

An index is especially valuable when it makes a conversation specific. Instead of presenting fifty pages and asking someone to find the problem, you can identify a particular item and explain the question it raises. The professional remains responsible for assessing its significance within their role. You remain responsible for preserving the originals and accurately describing your knowledge. Good organization is not about doing every specialized job yourself. It is about making collaboration easier without confusing your role with that of an attorney, investigator or forensic examiner.

16. Guided practice: organize three fictional records

Before using real information, practice with an invented situation. Create a fictional timeline with two events: an agreed appointment and a later change. Export it from the Incident Timeline Builder. Open the saved timeline in the Evidence Index so the tool can recognize the event identifiers. Create three evidence entries: an appointment notice, a message requesting the change and a message confirming the new time.

For each item, choose a neutral title, category and source status. Record its date if known and write a short description. Give the fictional source a storage alias rather than a real file path. Add a related timeline reference and note any limitation. Copy each generated evidence code back into the appropriate timeline entry during practice. Notice that a related event does not tell you whether the item independently establishes what happened.

Filter the index by category, export the full collection and open it in a fresh session. Verify that the records and their stable identifiers survive. Try entering an invalid event reference, then try a well-formed reference from a different timeline to understand how the tool distinguishes those cases. Edit an item and add a correction note. Finally, close the application and choose a task unrelated to documentation. The exercise is complete when you understand the system, its boundaries and how to leave it behind when the work is done.

17. Make the collection serve your life

Documentation can restore a sense of order when your circumstances feel unpredictable. It can also become a habit that keeps your attention fixed on conflict. Set a review interval and a realistic stopping point. If adding another item will not help answer an appropriate question or support a practical action, you may be better served by attending to work, family, sleep or another responsibility.

The purpose of the Hard Evidence phase is accuracy and preparation. It is not to monitor a former partner, collect embarrassing material or force someone to accept your interpretation. That distinction is central to the HAWK framework as used within Not Broken Just Burning. You can take legitimate concerns seriously while choosing conduct that reflects your own values.

When you are ready to move forward, use the index to identify what you already possess, what remains unknown and which question belongs with a qualified professional. Use the Flamewalker Path for the emotional and identity work that a document catalog cannot accomplish. Your own life deserves attention even if the conflict remains unresolved. A record is a tool. It does not need to become your identity, your source of worth or the center of your day. The next good decision may be as simple as closing your archive and spending time with your children.
